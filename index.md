# Introduction
This is a blog on the various topics I learned during my research experience.

In highschool Korean evolutionary theory textbooks, genetic drift is introduced as an event that happens by a founder effect or a bottleneck. What wasn't further explained was that all natural populations are of finite size, suggesting that genetic drift is prevalent in all evolution. This led to me being informed on the neutral theory of evolution, first proposed by Motoo Kimura and Jack Lester King with Thomas Hughes Jukes independently.
The modeling molecular evolution under a stochastic framework itself was such a big appeal for me that I set my future goals to study in such field.

Then I entered Seoul National University, enrolled in a Evolution class during my second year, and was introduced to formal population genomics. There, I met my PI up till my Master's degree, Choongwon Jeong. The class further motivated me to join his lab during my Bachelors.

I started my concrete research in population genomics at the Jeong Lab of Population Genomics at Seoul National University.
During my time there, I was introduced to population genomics theory such as the coalescent, diffusion theory (although I'm not that good at it), f-statistics, and different methodologies. I also had the chance to play around with genomic simulators, which I found most interesting.

To further set my career path of developing population genomics methodology and theory, I joined the University of Oregon for my PhD degree to study at the Kern Ralph co-lab. I hope I get to contribute to method development here, such as in tskit or other methodologies.

I always wanted to explain and make concrete of what I know of evolutionary theory and the mathematics behind it. I used to write some posts on my Korean blog, but I really never got with it anywhere. That's why I made this blog. This blog will serve as a folder binder for my popgen notes on classes and published papers on population genomics.

The goal of the page is as the following:
1. Try to be thorough and exact with the proofs and derivations
2. If possible, show codes and examples
3. Try not to gloss over anything: if I have to, at least cite the source of information or proof.
4. May add in some personal accounts of my research.

Overall, this site is much more of a personal project rather than something to be viewed in public. Let's see how this goes as we publish more blog posts.
